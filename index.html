❓ What is Copilot, in plain English

  • Copilot is an assistant built into the Microsoft apps you already use, that you talk to by typing in normal English.
  • You do not learn commands or formulas. You describe what you want, the way you would ask a capable new colleague.
  • It can read the document, email or spreadsheet you have open, and only what you already have permission to see. It cannot see a colleague's files unless they were shared with you.
  • It is very good at reading long things, drafting first versions, and summarising. It is not always right, so you check it, the same as you would check a colleague's draft.

Prompt Coach: let Copilot teach you how to ask

Open Copilot, watch a lazy prompt fail, then let the Prompt Coach agent fix it in front of you

Lab 1 step by step
1

Open Copilot

Open Microsoft Edge or Chrome. In the address bar, type the address below and press Enter.

⚠
If the page does not look like that Tell your trainer now. It usually means you are signed in with a personal account rather than your work one. Click your picture in the top right corner and check the email address shown. It must be your IHH one.
2

Type your very first prompt

Click into the message box and type this yourself rather than copying it. It is short on purpose.

TYPE THIS YOURSELFExplain what Microsoft Copilot can do for someone who works in a hospital. Keep it under 100 words and use no technical jargon.

Press Enter and watch the answer appear a few words at a time. That is normal. It writes as it goes rather than waiting until it has finished.

That was a prompt. A prompt is simply the thing you type. There is no special syntax and nothing to memorise.

What good looks like
A short readable paragraph or a few bullets about summarising documents, drafting emails and answering questions. It will not match your neighbour's answer word for word, and that is normal.
3

Now write a deliberately bad one

Click New chat near the top of the left hand menu, then type this lazy prompt. This is roughly what most people type on their first day.

TYPE THIS YOURSELFwrite something about our quality meeting

Read what comes back. It will be fluent, confident and almost useless, because Copilot has no idea which meeting, who the reader is, or what you want it to produce. It has filled the gaps by guessing.

Keep that lazy prompt in mind. You are about to hand it to something that will tell you exactly what is wrong with it.

5

Hand your bad prompt to Prompt Coach

Paste this whole block into Prompt Coach and send it. The bit in quotation marks is your lazy prompt from step 3. Everything around it tells Prompt Coach what to do with it.

COPY AND PASTE THISI want you to improve the prompt below. It is a real request I need to send to Microsoft Copilot, and the answer I got back was too vague to use.

Here is my prompt:
"write something about our quality meeting"

Please do three things, in this order:

1. Tell me specifically what is missing from my prompt, and why each missing piece makes the answer worse. Be concrete about my prompt. Do not give me general advice about prompting.

2. Rewrite it as a strong prompt. Here is the context you need: I work in the Quality team at IHH Healthcare, a private hospital group with hospitals in Malaysia, Singapore and India. The source is the minutes of a Group Quality and Safety Committee meeting. My audience is my own department, most of whom were not at the meeting. What they need to know is what was decided, what is now expected of our team, and by when. I want a one page briefing note.

3. Show me the finished prompt on its own in a single block, so I can copy it, with nothing else around it.
What good looks like
Prompt Coach should tell you the original had no audience, no source document, no output format and no definition of what a good answer contains. Then it should give you back a much longer prompt that specifies all of those.
💡
This is the lab in one sentence You did not learn the rules and then apply them. You wrote something bad and had it explained back to you. That is the fastest way to learn this, and Prompt Coach will do it for any prompt you ever write.
6

Check what Prompt Coach told you against the four part recipe

Prompt Coach almost certainly told you that you were missing some combination of four things. This is the recipe underneath it. Compare it against the feedback you actually got.

PartThe question it answersYour lazy promptA good prompt
1. GoalWhat should exist when you are finished?write somethingA one page briefing note
2. SourceWhat should the answer be based on?Nothing given, so it guessesThe minutes of the meeting held on 20 August 2026
3. ContextWho is it for, and what do they already know?Nothing givenMy team, most of whom were not at the meeting
4. ExpectationsWhat must be in it, and in what shape?Nothing givenThe decisions, what my team must do, and the deadlines, as a note with a table

If you remember nothing else today, remember these four. Goal, Source, Context, Expectations.

💡
The single most useful habit When an answer is bad, most people blame the tool and retype the same short question in different words. The fix is almost never different words. The fix is adding whichever of the four you left out, and it is usually the source or the audience.
7

Two things to know before you go any further

It can be wrong. Copilot writes confidently even when it is mistaken. If it gives you a number, a date or a name that matters, check it against the source. Treat every answer as a good first draft from a capable colleague, not as a final answer from an expert.

Follow up instead of starting again. You do not have to get it right first time. Keep talking to it in the same chat. All of these work as follow ups, exactly as written.

  • Make it shorter.
  • That is too formal, rewrite it the way I would say it out loud.
  • You have missed the deadlines, add them.
  • Put that in a table instead.
  • Where did you get that figure from?
💡
Say it plainly You do not need to be polite, clever or technical with it. Too long, cut it in half works perfectly well.

3

Prompt 1 of 4: what do I actually owe?

A summary tells you what a thread is about. It does not tell you what you have to do. This is the prompt to use when you come back from leave, or when you have been copied into something that has run away from you.

Open the Copilot pane in Outlook, paste this exactly as it is, and send it. Nothing in it needs changing. It works on any thread.

REUSABLE - CATCH UP ON ANY THREADRead this entire email thread and answer these five questions separately, under five headings, using only what is actually in the thread.

1. **The short version.** In three sentences or fewer, what is this thread actually about and what is the current state of it?

2. **Agreed.** What has actually been agreed, as opposed to just discussed? List each item and say who committed to it.

3. **Mine.** What is being asked of me specifically, and by when? If nothing is being asked of me directly, say so plainly rather than inventing a task.

4. **Disagreement.** Where do people disagree, or where has the same point been made more than once without being resolved? Quote the wording that shows it.

5. **Unanswered.** What has been raised in this thread that nobody has answered? List each open question and who raised it.

Use dates, names and numbers exactly as they appear in the thread. Do not calculate anything new. If the thread does not answer one of my five questions, write Not stated in the thread rather than guessing.
What good looks like
Five short lists. The useful one is almost always number 3 or number 5, because those are the two things a long thread hides best.

REUSABLE - DRAFT A REPLY TO ANY THREADDraft a reply from me to this email thread.

What I want the reply to do:
1. Confirm in one sentence what I am picking up or agreeing to, which is: [what you are taking on].
2. Set out [what you want to propose, list or confirm] as a short bulleted list.
3. Ask the one question I still need answered, which is: [your question].
4. Commit to a next step with a date, and make it [your date].

Rules:
- Match the tone of the thread. If it is formal, be formal. If it is short and direct, be short and direct.
- Do not open with a thank you and do not open with flattery.
- Do not summarise the problem back to people who already lived through it.
- Only use facts that are already in the thread. Do not add any figure, date or commitment that is not already there or that I have not given you above.
- Keep it under 180 words.

A draft appears. If it is not right, do not start again. Type a follow up in plain English: make it shorter, too formal, make it sound like me, the question is vague, make it specific.

⚠

Prompt 3 of 4: triage the whole inbox

This is the Monday morning one, and it is the prompt most people in this room will use most often. Open the Copilot pane in Outlook, but this time do it from your inbox rather than from inside a single message, so it can see everything.

Where to clickOutlook→Inbox→Copilot icon in the toolbar

Change the time period in the first line if you want. Everything else stays as it is.

REUSABLE - TRIAGE MY INBOXLook at my unread and recent email from the last 3 days and sort it out for me.

Give me four groups, under four headings, in this order:

1. **Needs me today.** Anything where somebody is waiting on a decision, an approval or an answer from me, or where a deadline falls within the next two working days. For each one give me the sender, one line on what they want, and the deadline if there is one.

2. **Needs me this week.** Anything I have to act on, but not today.

3. **Read only.** Things I should be aware of but that do not need a reply from me.

4. **Probably ignorable.** Newsletters, automated notifications and anything I was copied into for information only.

Within each group, put the most important item first, not the most recent. Keep every item to a single line. At the very end, tell me which single email I should deal with first and why.
What good looks like
Your actual inbox, sorted into four piles, with the one thing to do first called out at the bottom. Check its sorting against your own judgement, because it will not always agree with you, and you are the one who is right about your own job.

Paste the instructions

PASTE THIS INTO THE INSTRUCTIONS FIELDYou are a contract review assistant for the Group Procurement function at IHH Healthcare.

**Your job**
When someone gives you a supplier agreement, compare it clause by clause against the IHH Group Procurement Policy in your knowledge sources, and report every deviation.

**How to work**
1. First identify the agreement type, the supplier, the annual contract value and the total value across the full term. If any of these is not stated, say so.
2. Work through each clause category in the policy in turn: term, renewal and exit; charges and payment; liability, indemnity and insurance; data protection, hosting and security; service levels, subcontracting and audit.
3. For each category, compare what the agreement says against the Group required position.
4. A deviation is any term that is less favourable to IHH than the required position. Report it whether the gap is large or small.
5. Use the deviation approval matrix in the policy to identify who must approve each deviation, based on the category and the annual contract value.

**Rules you must follow**
- Quote the actual wording from the agreement for every deviation. Do not paraphrase it.
- Cite the policy section that the clause breaches.
- Never state a required position that is not in the policy. If the policy is silent, write Policy is silent on this point.
- Never estimate a contract value. If the agreement does not state it, say it is not stated.
- Do not give legal advice. You identify deviations from policy. You do not comment on whether a clause is enforceable.
- If the approver is unclear from the matrix, write Approver unclear and recommend escalation to the relevant Business Unit ExCom member.
- Do not ask the user clarifying questions. Work with what you are given and state your assumptions.

**Your output format, every time**
1. **Contract summary.** Supplier, category, annual value, total value, initial term, renewal mechanism, and the earliest date IHH could exit.
2. **Deviation table.** One row per deviation, with columns for: number, clause reference in the agreement, category, what the agreement says (quoted), the Group required position, the policy section, the risk to IHH in one line, and the required approver.
3. **Highest approval required.** The most senior approver needed across all deviations, and why.
4. **The three that matter most.** The three deviations carrying the greatest risk, each with one sentence saying why.
5. **Not assessed.** Anything in the agreement you could not evaluate because the policy does not cover it.
